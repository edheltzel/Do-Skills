## What it does

`writing-fragments` is the **explore** phase of writing: it interviews you relentlessly and mines the conversation for fragments, appending each to a single markdown file — without imposing any structure yet. Committing to a shape is *exploit*, and that's a different skill's job.

The defining constraint is that a fragment only has to be readable *by you* — it needn't define its terms or make sense to a cold reader. The bar is "is this a good piece of writing?", not "is this a self-contained argument?" You deliberately widen the space of what could be written before you narrow it.

## When to reach for it

You invoke this by typing `/writing-fragments` — it starts a piece from scratch when you have things to say but no structure yet. Reach for it at the very start of writing, when you want to get the raw material out. Once the pile is rich enough, shape it with [writing-drafting](./writing-drafting.md).

## The most valuable fragment

Fragments are deliberately heterogeneous — a sharp sentence, a vignette, a half-thought, a quote, a complaint. But the highest-value one to land is a **leading word**: a compact metaphor or coinage the whole piece can hang on (the way *tracer bullets* or *fog of war* names a whole pattern). Name the right one in explore and it shapes the structure, transitions, and title through the entire exploit phase. When the conversation circles a recurring idea, push to coin a word for it.

The rhythm is append-and-re-read: the agent appends silently (mentioning what it added in passing), and re-reads the file from disk before every write so your edits, reorders, and deletions are always preserved.

## Where it fits

The first half of a two-skill writing pipeline: `writing-fragments` (explore) feeds [writing-drafting](./writing-drafting.md) (exploit), which commits the fixed pile to a structure. Imported and adapted from Matt Pocock's skills (github.com/mattpocock/skills, MIT).
